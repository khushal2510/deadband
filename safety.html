What to carry out of the room

  • The action list, each item traced to a datasheet change, tag, I/O point or logic change.
  • Safeguards that are instrumented, so they appear in the C&E matrix and the SIL study scope.
  • Setpoints agreed in the room, recorded in the alarm and trip schedule with the reasoning.
  • Assumptions about bypasses, overrides and start-up inhibits.
  • Scenarios parked for LOPA, with the node and scenario number kept.

Safety lifecycle

  1. Hazard and risk assessment

    Find where a SIF is needed and the risk reduction it must deliver.

  2. Allocation of safety functions

    Decide which protection layers carry which risk reduction.

  3. Safety requirements specification

    Function, setpoints, response time, voting, safe state, SIL.

  4. Design and engineering of the SIS

    Select devices and architecture, verify the SIL.

  5. Installation, commissioning and validation

    FAT, SAT and end-to-end proof against the SRS.

  6. Operation and maintenance

    Proof tests, bypass control, demand and failure records.

  7. Modification

    Management of change returns to the right lifecycle step.

  8. Decommissioning

    Remove the function in a controlled way.

Source: IEC 61511-1 safety lifecycle. Functional safety management, verification and assessment apply throughout.

What an SRS holds per SIF

  • SIF ID, the hazardous event it prevents and the link to the HAZOP and LOPA scenario.
  • Required SIL, mode of operation and expected demand rate.
  • Process safety time and required response time, including valve stroke.
  • Trip setpoint, units, delay and measurement basis.
  • Safe state, de-energise or energise to trip, and reset philosophy.
  • Voting and architecture of sensors, logic solver and final elements.
  • Proof test interval and method, bypass and maintenance override rules.
  • Environmental limits and the requirements for diagnostics and fault alarms.

Source: IEC 61511-1 clause on safety requirements specification.

Alarm management

ISA-18.2 (IEC 62682) treats alarms as a lifecycle, so the setpoints you write in the alarm schedule end up in a managed system.

Lifecycle stages

  1. Philosophy
  2. Identification
  3. Rationalization
  4. Detailed design
  5. Implementation
  6. Operation
  7. Maintenance
  8. Monitoring and assessment
  9. Management of change
  10. Audit

Source: ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 lifecycle model.

Practical points

  • An alarm should signal a condition that needs an operator response, with a known consequence and enough time to act.
  • Every alarm needs a priority, setpoint, delay and a deadband so it does not chatter around the limit.
  • Alarms and trips are different things. A trip setpoint is normally further out than the alarm that warns of it.
  • Watch for alarm floods. A common definition is more than 10 alarms in 10 minutes for one operator.
  • Shelving, suppression and out-of-service states need clear rules and records.

Sources: ISA-18.2, EEMUA 191.
